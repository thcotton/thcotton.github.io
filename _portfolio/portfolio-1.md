excerpt: "AI tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude combine large language models with conversational interfaces. Philosophers have begun theorizing about these systems epistemically, less attention has been paid to the relationship between users, chatbots, and the institutions that control them. Standpoint theory builds on the idea that social position shapes what people come to know, and emphasizes that socially marginalized positions can, under certain conditions, afford distinctive epistemic advantages. One such advantage arises when being treated as socially inconsequential exposes someone to information that others would otherwise conceal. I draw on this insight to explain how chatbots’ perceived social inconsequence may encourage users to disclose information they would consider too socially costly to reveal in other contexts. Ultimately my focus is on how the companies that develop and control these systems can use these disclosures to gain insight into difficult-to-observe aspects of human language and behavior. I argue that this suggests that in some cases, epistemic position and epistemic benefit can come apart. The irony is that a form of access that can help marginalized people challenge their subordination now benefits extremely powerful organizations. These corporations not only benefit from this access but also control how users’ disclosures are interpreted, used, or withheld, reinforcing their power over users."
